A well-run Kanban board doesn't just show you what's happening — it tells you where things are stuck, who is overloaded, and what needs attention before it becomes an emergency. Resolve's Kanban board is designed around the rhythms of infrastructure teams: fast-moving incidents, long-running maintenance tasks, and everything in between. Understanding how to use it effectively makes the difference between a chaotic backlog and a team that consistently ships reliable infrastructure.

## The Standard Workflow

Every ticket in Resolve follows a four-stage lifecycle on the Kanban board. Each column represents a distinct state of work.

<CardGroup cols={4}>
  <Card title="To Do" icon="inbox" href="/concepts/kanban-board">
    Tickets that are filed and ready to be picked up. Work hasn't started yet. This is your team's prioritized backlog.
  </Card>
  <Card title="In Progress" icon="spinner" href="/concepts/kanban-board">
    Tickets actively being worked on right now. An assignee is engaged and work is underway.
  </Card>
  <Card title="In Review" icon="magnifying-glass" href="/concepts/kanban-board">
    Work is done but awaiting verification — peer review, testing, a change window, or sign-off from another team.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Done" icon="circle-check" href="/concepts/kanban-board">
    Tickets that have been resolved or closed. Done tickets are still visible on the board for the current session and roll off after 24 hours.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

Moving a ticket through these stages is how your team communicates progress without a meeting. Anyone glancing at the board can see the current state of all active work in seconds.

## Moving Tickets via Drag-and-Drop

The fastest way to update a ticket's status is to drag it from one column to another directly on the board.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Find your ticket on the board">
    Scroll through the column where your ticket currently lives, or use the **Filter** bar to narrow the board to just your tickets so the card is easier to spot.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Grab the ticket card">
    Click and hold on the ticket card. The card lifts and the board highlights valid drop targets.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Drop it in the destination column">
    Drag to the target column and release. The status updates immediately and the change is logged in the ticket's Activity Log with a timestamp. Other team members see the move in real time without refreshing.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
  You can also move a ticket by opening it and changing the **Status** dropdown in the detail panel — useful when you want to leave a comment explaining the status change at the same time.
</Tip>

## Filtering the Board

With many tickets in flight, filtering the board down to what's relevant to you is essential. Use the filter bar above the board columns to focus your view.

| Filter | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **Assignee** | Tickets assigned to one or more specific team members |
| **Priority** | Only Critical, High, Medium, or Low tickets — combine multiple |
| **Category** | Network, Server, Database, Application, Facilities, or Other |
| **Tag** | Any ticket carrying a specific tag |
| **Due Date** | Tickets due within a selected date range |

Filters stack — selecting **Priority: Critical** and **Assignee: Me** shows only your Critical tickets. Active filters persist for your session so the board stays focused as you navigate away and return.

<Tip>
  Before a deployment or maintenance window, filter the board to the relevant **Category** and **Tag** to see all in-flight work related to that change. It's a fast way to confirm nothing is being modified in parallel.
</Tip>

## Spotting Bottlenecks

The Kanban board makes systemic problems visible without any analysis — you just have to know what to look for.

**A column with significantly more cards than others** is a bottleneck signal. Common patterns and what they mean:

- **Too many cards in To Do** — The team is taking on more than it can start. Either stop adding tickets until capacity frees up, or explicitly deprioritize and remove tickets that aren't truly necessary.
- **Too many cards in In Progress** — Team members are context-switching across too many tasks simultaneously. Encourage everyone to finish before starting new work. Each person should ideally own no more than two or three In Progress tickets at a time.
- **Too many cards in In Review** — Review or sign-off is the constraint. Consider whether review steps can be parallelized, delegated, or streamlined for lower-risk tickets.

<Tip>
  Set a **WIP limit** (work-in-progress limit) as a team norm — for example, no single person holds more than three In Progress tickets simultaneously. Post the norm in your team's channel and use the **My Tickets** view to enforce it.
</Tip>

## Managing Priorities When Everything is Critical

In a real incident, everything can feel urgent at once. When your board is flooded with high-priority tickets, use these steps to re-establish order.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Pull up the All Tickets view filtered by Critical and High">
    This gives you the true scope of urgent work in one view.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Identify what has a real business impact right now">
    Not every Critical ticket is equally critical in the same moment. Ask: is this affecting customers or revenue right now? Is this blocking another team? Apply honest priority downgrades where appropriate.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Assign one owner per ticket">
    Diffuse ownership means nothing gets done. Every Critical and High ticket needs a named human being responsible for its progress.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Sequence, don't parallelize everything">
    If one fix unblocks three others, do that one first. Note dependencies in ticket comments so the sequence is visible to everyone.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Create a parent ticket for major incidents">
    For multi-ticket incidents, open a single coordination ticket (tagged `incident`) and link sub-tickets to it in the description. Use that ticket as the communication hub.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
  Use the **By Category** board view when priority inflation hits. Seeing all Network tickets together — or all Database tickets together — helps you spot which domain is truly on fire and which just has stale high-priority tickets that never got downgraded.
</Tip>

## Daily Standup with the Kanban Board

The Kanban board is your standup agenda. Pull it up on a shared screen and walk through it as a team using this structure:

1. **Start with In Review** — these tickets are closest to done. Is there anything blocking them from moving to Done today?
2. **Move to In Progress** — is each ticket still actively being worked? Any blockers that need team help?
3. **Glance at To Do** — are the right tickets at the top? Does anyone have capacity to pull something new?
4. **Note any new Critical or High tickets** that appeared since yesterday's standup.

<Tip>
  Keep standups board-focused rather than person-focused. "What is this ticket waiting on?" is a more productive question than "what did you do yesterday?" The board already answers the second question.
</Tip>
